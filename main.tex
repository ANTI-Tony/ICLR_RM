\begin{abstract}
Modern reasoning models---OpenAI's o-series, DeepSeek-R1, Claude with
extended thinking, and Qwen3-Thinking---allocate test-time compute
adaptively across tasks: tasks that look harder receive more thinking
tokens, easier ones stop earlier. Recent work on adaptive test-time
compute codifies this difficulty-based heuristic into learned routers
and confidence-driven early-stopping policies. The implicit
assumption underlying all of these methods is that compute buys
accuracy, and that accuracy is fungible across tasks.

This assumption breaks in real deployment. Behind a given accuracy,
the ``remaining error'' carries wildly different costs across tasks:
a typo in a log message and a database migration that silently
corrupts a production table both count as a single unit of accuracy
on a benchmark, but only one of them is worth additional compute to
avoid. We argue that test-time compute should be allocated by
expected cost-of-error, not by predicted difficulty.

We propose \emph{consequence-aware} test-time compute allocation. At
its core is a lightweight predictor that estimates the deployment-time
cost-of-error of each task directly from its issue text; a scheduler
then routes higher-consequence tasks to larger compute tiers---or
larger thinking budgets---under the same total budget as a
difficulty-based baseline.

We evaluate on 700 software-engineering tasks (SWE-bench Lite and
Multi-SWE-bench mini), labeled under three independent pipelines, and
audit three contemporary thinking models. Across all labeling
pipelines, consequence is statistically orthogonal to difficulty and
remains a significant predictor of cost-weighted gain after
controlling for difficulty and cross-model confidence. Current
thinking models do not allocate compute by consequence: their spending
is either uncorrelated with consequence, saturated at the token cap,
or weakly leaning but far below the required magnitude. Our
issue-only predictor never misclassifies a high-consequence task as
low-consequence in 300 SWE-bench tasks. Under matched compute budgets,
consequence-aware routing reduces cost-weighted loss by more than
$30\%$ relative to difficulty-aware routing, and the predictor-driven
variant retains over $90\%$ of the oracle gain. Notably,
difficulty-aware routing performs worse than random because the
hardest tasks are unsolvable at any tier.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Modern reasoning models---OpenAI's o-series, DeepSeek-R1, Anthropic's
Claude with extended thinking, and Qwen3-Thinking---vary how many
tokens they spend per task. Whether through learned
routers~\citep{damani2024learning}, compute-difficulty scaling
laws~\citep{snell2024scaling}, or confidence-driven early
stopping~\citep{manvi2024adaptive}, recent work on adaptive test-time
compute converges on a single heuristic: \emph{spend more on harder
tasks}.

The implicit assumption underlying this heuristic is that extra
compute is purchased to raise expected accuracy, and that accuracy
is fungible across tasks. This assumption holds on benchmarks---where
each task contributes a single fail/pass increment to a global
score---but breaks in real deployment. Consider two failures a
coding assistant might produce on the same day: a typo in a log
message, and a database migration that silently corrupts a production
table under a race condition. On a benchmark, both count as one
failed task. In deployment, only the second is worth additional
compute to avoid. The cost of the remaining error is wildly
heterogeneous across tasks, and a scheduler that treats them all the
same is leaving most of its risk-reduction budget on the floor.

We argue that test-time compute should be allocated by expected
cost-of-error, not by predicted difficulty. Formally, the deployment
objective is not the maximize-accuracy-subject-to-budget formulation
\[
\max_{\pi}\ \mathrm{acc}(\pi) - \lambda\,c(\pi),
\]
adopted by current adaptive-compute work, but a cost-weighted risk
minimization
\begin{equation}\label{eq:objective}
c^{\ast}(x) \;=\; \arg\min_{c}\ \alpha\!\cdot\!c \;+\; \mathrm{cost}(x)\cdot P(\mathrm{err}\mid x, c),
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{cost}(x)$ is the realized loss if the model gets task
$x$ wrong. The closest prior framing---rational
metareasoning~\citep{russell1991right, hay2012selecting,
yang2024rational}---already includes a utility term in its
decision-theoretic foundation, but modern LLM operationalizations
implicitly take $\mathrm{cost}(x)\equiv 1$, collapsing the objective
back to a difficulty-only regime.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_motivation.png}
\caption{\textbf{Three contemporary thinking models fail to allocate
compute by consequence.} Each panel shows one model's relationship
between actual thinking length and our consequence label on
SWE-bench Lite tasks. Qwen3-8B's thinking length is uncorrelated with
consequence ($\rho = 0.002$). Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking pins $99.3\%$ of
tasks at its 8192-token cap. Claude Sonnet 4.5 with extended thinking
shows a statistically significant but quantitatively inadequate
leaning ($\rho = 0.20$, $p = 0.008$, $n = 171$). Across three models
with three different scheduling mechanisms, none allocates compute by
consequence at the magnitude rational decision-making requires.}
\label{fig:motivation}
\end{figure}

An intuitive defense of the difficulty-default would be that strong
reasoning models, even without an explicit cost signal, might allocate
compute by consequence emergently. Figure~\ref{fig:motivation} reports
the audit. Across three contemporary thinking models with three
different scheduling mechanisms, none allocates compute by consequence
at the magnitude rational decision-making requires: Qwen3-8B's
thinking length is essentially uncorrelated with consequence
($\rho = 0.002$), Qwen3-VL-8B-Thinking pins $99.3\%$ of tasks at its
8192-token cap, and Claude Sonnet 4.5 shows a statistically
significant but quantitatively inadequate leaning ($\rho = 0.20$,
$p = 0.008$, $n = 171$; high-consequence tasks receive only $17\%$
more thinking than low-consequence ones---an order of magnitude below
what Equation~\eqref{eq:objective} would prescribe).

We propose \emph{consequence-aware test-time compute allocation}. A
lightweight predictor estimates the deployment-time cost-of-error of
each task directly from its issue text. A scheduler then routes
higher-consequence tasks to larger compute tiers---or, equivalently,
larger thinking budgets---under the same total compute budget as a
difficulty-based baseline. No reasoning model is retrained; the
intervention sits entirely at the scheduling layer.

The remainder of the paper builds the empirical case in five steps.
\begin{itemize}
\item[(1)] \textbf{Consequence is statistically independent of
difficulty, and is not a re-skinned confidence signal.} On 700
software-engineering tasks (300 SWE-bench Lite and 400
Multi-SWE-bench mini) labeled under three independent
pipelines---rule-based, LLM-with-patch, and LLM-issue-only---Spearman
$\rho(\mathrm{consequence}, \mathrm{difficulty}) \approx 0$ holds
under every labeling. Partialing out difficulty \emph{and}
cross-model pass-rate variance (a confidence proxy), consequence
still explains an additional $26.4$ percentage points of cost-weighted
marginal value ($+55.7\%$ relative; \S\ref{sec:cons}).

\item[(2)] \textbf{Existing thinking models do not allocate by
consequence}, in three distinct failure modes
(\S\ref{sec:audit}, Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}): no signal
(Qwen3-8B), saturation (Qwen3-VL-Thinking), and emergent-but-inadequate
leaning (Claude Sonnet 4.5).

\item[(3)] \textbf{Consequence can be predicted from issue text alone,
and predicted safely for deployment.} A Claude-issue-only predictor
agrees with the with-patch reference at Cohen's $\kappa = 0.572$
(within-model) and $\kappa = 0.379$ (cross-model). Crucially, in
$300$ SWE-bench tasks the predictor \emph{never} misclassifies a
high-consequence task as low-consequence ($\mathrm{class}\,2 \to
\mathrm{class}\,0$ error rate $= 0\%$): even when wrong, it errs to
the adjacent class, never across the safety gap
(\S\ref{sec:pred}).

\item[(4)] \textbf{Consequence-aware allocation reduces cost-weighted
loss by over $30\%$ relative to difficulty-aware under matched total
compute,} on a $16$-model SWE-bench compute-tier benchmark; the
predictor-driven variant retains over $90\%$ of the oracle gain
(\S\ref{sec:method}, \S\ref{sec:exp}).

\item[(5)] \textbf{Difficulty-aware allocation is in fact worse than
random,} and we surface the root cause:
$\rho(\mathrm{difficulty}, \Delta\mathrm{success}) = -0.78$, meaning
the hardest tasks have the \emph{lowest} marginal gain from extra
compute---they are unsolvable at any tier. A top-$25\%$ selection
ranked by difficulty captures \emph{zero percent} of the
cost-weighted gain available in the data, versus $43\%$ for
consequence-ranked and $24\%$ for random
(\S\ref{sec:exp}).
\end{itemize}
