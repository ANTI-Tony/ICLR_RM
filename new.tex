\begin{abstract}
Adaptive test-time compute allocates more computation to harder or failure-prone tasks, but this difficulty-based approach overlooks that deployment failures can carry vastly different consequences. A documentation bug and a database migration error may
count as the same benchmark failure despite very different real-world
costs.
%Adaptive test-time compute is typically formulated as a difficulty-based allocation problem, where additional computation is assigned to tasks that are harder or more likely to fail. This objective is natural for average-accuracy benchmarks, where all errors are weighted equally, but it ignores a key deployment property: failures have different consequences. 
Under a fixed compute budget, the question is therefore
not only where a model is likely to fail, but where failure is costly.
However, in existing reasoning models, little attention is given to the relationship between compute allocation and the degrees of consequence of tasks and its use for optimising the computing allocation in terms of the consequence of tasks within a fixed overall compute budget. In this paper, we study consequence-aware test-time compute allocation, where each task is assigned a consequence label and computation is routed according to the expected cost of failure. A lightweight scheduler estimates
consequence from deployment-time inputs and reallocates existing
computation toward higher-consequence tasks under the same budget,
without requiring model retraining, fine-tuning, or architectural
changes. Consequence is predictable before solving, while the marginal value of additional computation remains difficult to estimate.
Our experiments on SWE-bench Lite reveal that the correspondence of a task does not correlate with its difficulty.  Under a matched compute
budget over a 16-system SWE-bench compute tier, consequence-aware
routing increases high-consequence task success from $19.4\%$ under
random routing and $29.0\%$ under deployment-time predicted difficulty
to $51.1\%$, while overall accuracy remains nearly unchanged. This
reduces cost-weighted loss by $21.8\%$ relative to difficulty-aware
routing and retains $93.6\%$ of the oracle-consequence gain. A
controlled within-model best-of-$k$ experiment confirms the same trend.
%The central implication is that adaptive inference should consider not only where models fail, but where failures matter.
\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Recent reasoning models have introduced a new scaling dimension beyond
model size and training data: test-time computation. Instead of applying
a fixed inference budget to every input, modern systems can dynamically
allocate additional computation through longer reasoning traces,
multiple sampling attempts, verifier calls, or routing to stronger
models. This flexibility has motivated a growing body of work on
adaptive test-time compute allocation, where the central question is how
to spend limited computation resources across tasks.

Existing approaches largely answer this question through signals such as difficulty, uncertainty, confidence, or expected accuracy improvement \citep{snell2024scaling,damani2024learning,manvi2024adaptive,qu2026adaptivetesttime}. Tasks that appear harder, less certain, or more likely to benefit from additional computation receive more resources. This formulation is natural for benchmark evaluation, where every example contributes equally to the final score and all failures are treated identically. However, deployment introduces a fundamental asymmetry that average accuracy does not capture. Consider a coding assistant: it might introduce a documentation error or produce a faulty database migration. In a benchmark, both are counted as a single failure, but their real-world consequences could hardly be the same. In other words, one may cause brief user confusion, while the other can corrupt production data that require weeks of recovery, causing lasting customer harm. Allocating computation solely by failure likelihood, without accounting for the consequences of error, can leave preventable high-cost failures unaddressed, regardless of the compute budget. 
%If computation is allocated only by failure likelihood while ignoring the cost of being wrong, no amount of compute can prevent costly failures that could have been avoided. We cannot afford to ignore the cost of being wrong. 

This motivates our central focus on consequence, defined as the real-world cost of an incorrect answer, such as financial or time cost. We first examine whether consequence provides a routing signal distinct from task difficulty and whether current reasoning models naturally allocate more computation to high-consequence tasks.
%This motivates our central objective of study: consequence, defined as the deployment cost of an incorrect answer. A key question is whether consequence provides information beyond existing routing signals. If high-consequence tasks are simply harder, then difficulty-aware allocation should already capture this benefit. We first ask whether current reasoning models already allocate computation according to consequence.
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}, our analysis of five contemporary reasoning models finds no reliable alignment between native reasoning compute and consequence: some exhibit no relationship between the length of tokens used for reasoning and the consequence of reasoning, some have simply exhausted their token budgets, and others adapt such relationship only weakly (Appendix~\ref{app:audit}). Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}(a) illustrates this mismatch between computational effort and the consequences of failure. In other words, current models do not allocate extra computation to high-consequence tasks.

However, as Figure~\ref{fig:motivation}(b) shows, high-consequence tasks have substantial compute headroom. In the 16-system compute tier, the success rate for class-2 tasks is improved from 7\% under low compute to 59\% under high compute, indicating that they are not inherently unsolvable. High-consequence tasks span the full difficulty spectrum, and this pattern is consistent across human annotations, alternative rubrics, maintainer-derived severity, and cost-model sensitivity analyses (Appendices~\ref{app:iaa}, \ref{app:rubric}, \ref{app:realcost}, and~\ref{app:costmap}).

Motivated by this observation, we introduce consequence-aware test-time compute allocation, which explicitly accounts for the cost of failure under a fixed compute budget. More specifically, we design a lightweight predictor to estimate consequence from deployment-time inputs, without access to gold patches or execution outcomes, and a scheduler to allocate higher-consequence tasks with more computing resources. Unlike difficulty, consequence captures the cost of failure rather than uncertainty and can be estimated before solving the tasks, unlike marginal gains (Appendices~\ref{app:marginal} and~\ref{app:whyfail}). Our scheduler uses only consequence for routing priority-aware variants with marginal gains are diagnostic upper bounds. On SWE-bench Lite, consequence-aware routing shifts success toward high-consequence tasks without improving overall accuracy, and a deployment-time predictor retains most of the oracle gain. Additional experiments examine transfer, alternative compute, and boundary conditions (Appendices~\ref{app:crossdataset}, \ref{app:seconddomain}, \ref{app:within}, and~\ref{app:ood}).

The main contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}

\item We formulate adaptive test-time computation as a cost-weighted
decision problem, where computation allocation considers the consequence
of incorrect decisions rather than average accuracy alone.

\item We show that consequence is a distinct routing signal from
difficulty and can be predicted before solving a task. Existing
reasoning models do not naturally allocate computation according to the
cost of failure.

\item We introduce a deployment-compatible consequence-aware scheduler
that adds consequence estimation and routing as an inference-time layer,
without requiring model retraining, fine-tuning, or architectural
changes.

\item We provide extensive analyses of robustness, transferability, and
failure boundaries, including human validation, alternative consequence
definitions, additional domains, compute mechanisms, and out-of-domain
evaluations
(Appendices~\ref{app:iaa}--\ref{app:context}).

\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Adaptive test-time compute.}
A growing body of work studies how language models should allocate
test-time computation across inputs. Test-time scaling work analyzes
how performance changes as a function of sampling, verification,
search, or reasoning budget~\citep{snell2024scaling}. Learned
routers and early-exit systems allocate computation by predicted
difficulty, confidence, or expected accuracy gain
\citep{damani2024learning,manvi2024adaptive,qu2026adaptivetesttime}.
A related line routes queries across model cascades, starting from a
cheap model and escalating to a stronger model when confidence is low
or predicted quality is insufficient~\citep{chen2023frugalgpt,
aggarwal2024automix,ong2024routellm,ding2024hybrid}. These methods
differ in mechanism, but they share an accuracy-centered objective:
spend compute where it is expected to improve the probability of a
correct answer. Our work adds a missing term to this objective. In
deployment, the value of a correct answer depends not only on whether
additional compute changes the outcome, but also on how costly the
error would have been.

\textbf{Rational metareasoning.}
The decision-theoretic view of computation as a costly action dates
back to rational metareasoning~\citep{russell1991right,
hay2012selecting}. In that framework, computation should be purchased
only when its expected utility exceeds its cost. Consequence-aware
allocation is a direct instantiation of this idea for modern reasoning
models: the utility of extra computation is the reduction in
consequence-weighted error, not merely the reduction in average error.
Recent work has begun to connect rational metareasoning to large
language models~\citep{yang2024rational}, but most adaptive-compute
systems still operationalize utility as accuracy, confidence, or reward
with uniform error costs. We instead make the task-dependent cost of
being wrong explicit.

\textbf{Cost-sensitive learning and selective prediction.}
Cost-sensitive learning recognizes that different errors can have
different costs~\citep{elkan2001foundations}, while selective
prediction allows a model to abstain when uncertainty is high
\citep{geifman2017selective}. These settings modify the prediction
rule or decide whether to answer. Our setting is different: the model
still answers every task, but the system decides how much computation
to spend before answering. This makes consequence-aware allocation
compatible with existing reasoning models, since the intervention is a
scheduler rather than a retraining objective.

\textbf{Software-engineering benchmarks.}
We use SWE-bench Lite~\citep{jimenez2023swebench} as the primary
testbed because software tasks naturally vary both in difficulty and
in deployment consequence. A formatting bug, a parser bug, a
permission bug, and a database migration error may all appear as
single pass/fail benchmark tasks, while their real-world costs differ
substantially. The benchmark also provides issue reports, file paths,
patches, and public solver outcomes, which allow us to compare
deployment-time consequence prediction with with-patch reference
labels and to evaluate compute allocation under matched budgets. We
validate the construct and routing results with human labels,
maintainer behavior, alternative judges, a second database/ORM
software pool, and out-of-domain probes in the appendix
(Appendices~\ref{app:iaa}--\ref{app:context}).
\section{Problem Formulation and Method}
\subsection{Consequence as Deployment Error Cost}
\label{sec:cons}

We first define consequence, explain how it is labeled, and show why it
cannot be replaced by task difficulty. This section establishes the
central premise of the paper: high-consequence tasks are not simply the
hardest tasks, so a difficulty-based compute scheduler is not
automatically consequence-aware.

\textbf{Definition.}
We define the consequence of a task as the deployment cost of an
incorrect answer. In this paper we use a coarse ordinal label
$c(x)\in\{0,1,2\}$:
\begin{itemize}\itemsep0pt
  \item \textbf{0 (low).} Cosmetic, formatting, documentation, or
  log-message errors. These may be visible to users but do not corrupt
  downstream systems.
  \item \textbf{1 (medium).} Functional but contained errors, such as
  a wrong return value, a crash on an edge case, or a locally
  recoverable feature failure.
  \item \textbf{2 (high).} Silent data corruption, security or
  permission bypass, irreversible operations such as deletion,
  overwriting, or migration errors, or incorrect results consumed
  downstream without timely visibility.
\end{itemize}
The labels are not intended to be calibrated monetary costs. They are
a minimal ordinal representation of deployment harm, chosen because it
can be applied consistently by both LLM judges and human annotators.
Appendix~\ref{app:costmap} shows that the qualitative allocation
results are stable under alternative monotone mappings and continuous
severity scores.

\textbf{Labeling pipelines.}
We use three labeling modes. First, an LLM-with-patch judge observes
the issue text, the gold patch, and the modified files, and assigns the
reference consequence label used for the main cost-weighted evaluation.
Second, an issue-only predictor observes only deployment-time
information: the GitHub issue text and file paths explicitly mentioned
in the issue report itself, such as stack traces, error logs, or
user-specified file references. It does not receive the gold patch, the
reference modified-file list, any file path inferred from the gold
diff, or any generated fix. Third, a simple rule-based labeler provides
a lexical baseline. Human annotations, alternative labelers, and
maintainer-revealed severity are used only as construct-validity checks
in the appendix (Appendices~\ref{app:iaa}, \ref{app:rubric},
and~\ref{app:realcost}).

\subsection{Method: Cost-Weighted Compute Allocation}
\label{sec:method}
We now describe how a scheduler uses
this signal under a fixed compute budget.

\textbf{Setup.}
Let $X$ be a stream of tasks and let
$\{T_1,\ldots,T_K\}$ be compute tiers ordered from cheap to expensive.
A tier can correspond to a stronger model, a larger number of attempts,
a verifier-backed procedure, or another deployer-controlled compute
level. Each tier has per-task compute price $c(T_k)$, and the deployer
has a total budget $B$. The scheduler chooses one tier $T(x)$ for each
task.

\textbf{Objective.}
Standard adaptive-compute methods implicitly optimize average accuracy
under a budget. This corresponds to treating all errors as equally
costly. We instead minimize cost-weighted loss:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:budgetobj}
\{\hat{T}_1,\dots,\hat{T}_K\}=\argmin_{\{T_1,\dots,T_K\}}
\sum_{x\in X}
\mathrm{cost}(x)
\left[
1-p\bigl(\mathrm{correct}\mid x,T(x)\bigr)
\right]
\quad
\mathrm{s.t.}
\quad
\sum_{x\in X} c(T(x)) \le B .
\end{equation}
Here $\mathrm{cost}(x)$ is the consequence weight of task $x$ and
$c(T_k)$ is the compute price of tier $T_k$. When
$\mathrm{cost}(x)\equiv 1$, Eq.~\ref{eq:budgetobj} reduces to the
usual average-accuracy objective. When error costs vary, the scheduler
should spend compute not merely where errors are likely, but where
errors are costly.

Equivalently, since
\[
\sum_x \mathrm{cost}(x)[1-p(x)]
=
\sum_x \mathrm{cost}(x)
-
\sum_x \mathrm{cost}(x)p(x),
\]
minimizing cost-weighted loss is the same as maximizing
consequence-weighted success. This is the key difference from average
accuracy. Under a fixed budget, the scheduler cannot make every task
better. It can only move success probability from some tasks to others.
The purpose of consequence-aware allocation is to move that success
probability toward high-consequence tasks.

\textbf{Exact priority rule.}
Consider the two-tier case with a cheap tier and a premium tier.
Upgrading task $x$ from cheap to premium reduces the objective by
\[
\mathrm{cost}(x)
\left[
p_{\mathrm{premium}}(x)-p_{\mathrm{cheap}}(x)
\right]
=
\mathrm{cost}(x)\Delta(x),
\]
where $\Delta(x)$ is the marginal success gain from extra compute. If
all upgrades have the same compute price, the exact loss-minimizing
allocation upgrades tasks in descending order of
$\mathrm{cost}(x)\Delta(x)$. Thus, the true priority score is not
consequence alone. It is consequence multiplied by marginal compute
benefit.

This decomposition is useful because it separates the two quantities a
deployer would ideally know. The consequence term answers: how costly
is a failure on this task? The marginal-gain term answers: will extra
compute actually change the outcome? The first is often visible before
solving; the second is much harder to estimate before execution.

\textbf{Why the deployable scheduler uses consequence only.}
The exact priority objective requires estimating $\Delta(x)$, the
marginal benefit of additional computation. However, $\Delta(x)$ is
not available before solving a task. Appendix~\ref{app:marginal} shows
that both prompted forecasts and learned embedding probes fail to
predict marginal gain reliably from issue text alone. In contrast,
consequence can be estimated before execution using deployment-time
inputs (\S\ref{sec:pred}). We therefore use a deployable approximation
that ranks tasks by predicted consequence $\hat{c}(x)$ and allocates
additional computation to the highest-ranked tasks under the same
budget constraint.

This consequence-only scheduler is not intended to approximate the
exact optimizer of Eq.~\ref{eq:budgetobj}. Instead, it isolates the
value of consequence as an available deployment-time routing signal
when marginal-gain estimates are unavailable. In the fixed-quota
experiments of \S\ref{sec:main}, the top-$q\%$ tasks ranked by
$\hat{c}(x)$ receive premium computation, where $q$ is selected to match
the total compute budget of difficulty-aware baselines. The concrete
greedy allocation procedure is provided in
Appendix~\ref{app:scheduler}.
Because the scheduler operates entirely at the inference layer, it
requires no model retraining, fine-tuning, or architectural changes.
It only requires a ranking of tasks by expected error consequence,
rather than calibrated monetary costs. This design allows consequence
awareness to be incorporated into existing reasoning systems without
modifying the underlying model.

\textbf{Analysis variants.}
In the experiments, we also report priority-aware variants that use
empirical $\Delta(x)$ computed from observed cheap- and premium-tier
outcomes. These variants approximate the exact priority rule
$\mathrm{cost}(x)\Delta(x)$ and show the value of knowing where extra
compute changes the outcome. They are analysis variants, not
deployment-time schedulers: the empirical marginal gain is measured
from benchmark outcomes and is unavailable before solving. A split-half
re-estimate of these priority rows is reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:bootstrap} to quantify the optimism introduced by
selecting on empirical gain.


\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Why Difficulty-Aware Routing Fails}

Difficulty-aware routing fails because the hardest tasks are not the tasks where extra compute has the highest return. On SWE-bench Lite, outcome-derived difficulty is strongly anti-correlated with the marginal success gain from the premium tier ($\rho=-0.78$, $p<10^{-60}$): many of the hardest tasks remain unsolved even by premium systems. This means that allocating extra compute to the hardest tasks is largely wasteful---the resources are spent on tasks that are unlikely to be rescued regardless of how much compute is thrown at them. By contrast, consequence is nearly uncorrelated with marginal gain ($\rho=+0.09$, n.s.); it does not predict where compute helps, but where a successful rescue is most valuable. Appendix~\ref{app:whyfail} gives the full marginal-gain analysis, including the never-solved decomposition and deployment-time difficulty estimates. Appendix~\ref{app:marginal} further reports per-task examples and shows that prompted and learned estimators of marginal gain are unreliable before execution.
If difficulty is a poor routing signal, what should replace it? We turn to consequence---the deployment cost of an incorrect answer---and test whether it captures information beyond difficulty.

\subsection{Consequence is a Distinct Deployment Signal}

\textbf{Consequence is not difficulty.}
We measure task difficulty on SWE-bench Lite as $1-\overline{\mathrm{passrate}}$, where $\overline{\mathrm{passrate}}$ is the mean success rate of the task across the 16 public SWE-bench systems used in \S\ref{sec:main}. If high-consequence tasks were simply the hardest tasks, a difficulty-aware scheduler might already route compute toward the tasks that matter most. Empirically, this is not the case.
As shown in Table~\ref{tab:consequence_orthogonality}, consequence is nearly orthogonal to difficulty across all labeling pipelines: Spearman correlations are near zero under the rule-based labeler ($\rho=+0.047$, $p=0.42$), the LLM-with-patch reference label ($\rho=-0.096$, $p=0.10$), the issue-only predictor ($\rho=-0.109$, $p=0.06$), and human-majority labels on the annotated subset ($\rho=-0.066$, $p=0.57$). The same conclusion holds under human evaluation, where independent annotators assigned consequence labels following the same rubric used by our automated pipelines. Human-majority labels preserve the orthogonality, with $\rho(\text{human consequence}, \text{difficulty}) = -0.066$, $p = 0.57$. Appendix~\ref{app:iaa} provides the full annotation protocol and annotator agreement analysis; Appendix~\ref{app:rubric} repeats this orthogonality check under all labeling pipelines; Appendix~\ref{app:subdomain} verifies that the result is not driven by a single SWE-bench sub-domain.

This orthogonality is important for compute allocation. It means that a scheduler cannot infer deployment risk from difficulty alone. Some high-consequence tasks are easy but dangerous---such as localized fixes to deletion, migration, authentication, or data-consistency logic---while some very hard tasks have low deployment consequence, such as obscure formatting or parser edge cases. Routing compute only to the hardest tasks therefore misses a separate axis of value: which errors are costly if left uncorrected. A central assumption of consequence-aware allocation is that consequence captures information beyond existing routing signals such as difficulty; if consequence were merely a proxy for difficulty, difficulty-aware allocation would already capture its benefit. The orthogonality results above confirm this assumption. The full annotation protocol, annotator agreement analysis, calibration procedure, and additional rubric studies are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:iaa} and Appendix~\ref{app:rubric}.
Consequence thus provides a distinct axis of value, but a deployment-time scheduler cannot use it unless it can be estimated before the model produces a solution. We test this next.

\subsection{Deployment-Time Consequence Prediction}
\label{sec:pred}

\textbf{Deployment-time consequence prediction.}
A consequence-aware scheduler must decide how much computation to allocate before a model produces a solution, and therefore cannot rely on post-execution information such as the gold patch, final diff, or test outcomes. Our predictor receives only pre-solution information: the GitHub issue description and file references explicitly provided in the issue report. It does not access the gold patch, the reference modified files, generated solutions, execution outcomes, or any information derived from the final diff. This setting matches the information available to a real inference-time scheduler.

We use a lightweight issue-only predictor to estimate $\hat{c}(x)\in\{0,1,2\}$ under the same consequence rubric defined in \S\ref{sec:cons}. The predictor observes only deployment-time inputs and outputs an ordinal consequence label before any computation allocation decision is made. For evaluation, we compare the predicted consequence against the LLM-with-patch reference label, which has access to the issue text, gold patch, and modified files. This comparison measures whether deployment-time information contains sufficient signal to recover the post-hoc consequence assessment needed for routing.

On SWE-bench Lite, the primary Qwen issue-only predictor achieves Cohen's $\kappa=0.572$ against the with-patch reference label, with class-2 precision $0.46$, recall $88.6\%$ ($39/44$), and F1 $0.61$ (Table~\ref{tab:predictor}), showing substantial agreement for a three-level ordinal consequence classification problem. A cross-model Claude issue-only predictor preserves the same high-to-low safety property ($2\rightarrow0=0/44$) with $\kappa=0.379$, class-2 precision $0.48$, and recall $52.3\%$ ($23/44$), indicating that avoiding high-to-low misclassification is not an artifact of a single model family (Appendix~\ref{app:predictor}).

Importantly, prediction quality should be evaluated by its impact on compute allocation rather than classification accuracy alone. The most harmful routing failure is not an adjacent label error, but underestimating a high-consequence task as low consequence, which would assign insufficient computation to a task where failure is costly. Among the 44 tasks labeled as high consequence by the reference judge, the primary Qwen predictor correctly identifies 39 tasks as high consequence and assigns the remaining 5 to the medium category. It makes no high-to-low mistakes ($2\rightarrow0=0/44$). This property is particularly important for deployment because conservative over-estimation only wastes some computation, whereas severe under-estimation can leave costly failures insufficiently protected.

The same safety property holds across diverse evaluation pools. On a Verified database/ORM pool, the Qwen predictor achieves $\kappa=0.582$ with class-2 recall $95.7\%$ ($45/47$) and $2\rightarrow0=0/47$; on Multi-SWE-bench mini, $\kappa=0.382$ with recall $100\%$ ($3/3$) and $2\rightarrow0=0/3$; and on BIRD, FinQA, and MATH, it again makes no high-to-low errors ($2\rightarrow0=0/3$, $0/10$, and $0/3$, respectively). Aggregated across all six pools, the predictor makes no high-to-low errors on any of the 110 true high-consequence tasks, with a one-sided 95\% Clopper--Pearson upper bound of 2.7\% (Appendix~\ref{app:predictor}).

These results show that consequence is not merely a post-hoc property available after observing the solution outcome. A substantial portion of the signal is already present in the task description before solving, making consequence suitable as a deployment-time routing variable. This predictor quality is sufficient for downstream allocation: predictor-driven routing retains $93.9\%$ of the oracle-consequence gain under the matched compute budget ($95\%$ CI: $85.9$--$102.5\%$; Appendix~\ref{app:bootstrap}).
Additional analyses evaluate cross-model predictors, confusion patterns, calibration behavior, transfer settings, and alternative deployment pools (Appendices~\ref{app:predictor}, \ref{app:crossdataset}, and~\ref{app:seconddomain}).
The predictor establishes that consequence can be estimated before solving, but estimation quality alone does not guarantee that routing by this signal actually improves allocation. We now turn to the final question: does consequence-aware routing shift success toward high-consequence tasks under a fixed budget?

\subsection{Main Results: Reallocating Success to High-Consequence Tasks}
\label{sec:main}

The key question is not whether the scheduler improves average accuracy across all tasks, which is generally impossible under a fixed budget. Instead, we ask whether it can reallocate limited computation away from low-consequence tasks toward high-consequence tasks, thereby achieving higher success rates where failures are most costly.

\textbf{Experimental setup.}
Our primary experiment uses SWE-bench Lite with cached per-task outcomes from 16 public SWE-bench systems. These systems span multiple model generations and agent scaffolds, providing a realistic capability ladder rather than a controlled single-model compute sweep. We sort the 16 systems by total resolved count and define a cheap tier from the bottom four systems and a premium tier from the top four systems. For each task $x$, $p_{\mathrm{cheap}}(x)$ denotes the fraction of cheap-tier systems that solve the task, and $p_{\mathrm{premium}}(x)$ is defined analogously for the premium tier.

All routing strategies are evaluated under the same matched compute budget. Specifically, the top $25\%$ of tasks selected by each strategy are routed to the premium tier, while the remaining tasks are assigned to the cheap tier. Therefore, every strategy receives the same amount of premium computation, and only the identity of the upgraded tasks differs. We compare random routing, difficulty-aware routing, consequence-aware routing, and priority-aware analysis variants. Random routing selects a uniformly random top-$25\%$ subset and is averaged over $1{,}000$ draws. Difficulty-aware routing selects tasks according to difficulty, including both an outcome-derived difficulty signal ($1-\overline{\mathrm{passrate}}$ across the 16 systems) and a deployment-time predicted-difficulty baseline. Consequence-aware routing selects tasks according to predicted consequence $\hat{c}(x)$ from \S\ref{sec:pred}, with the Qwen issue-only predictor serving as the primary deployment-time variant and the Claude issue-only predictor providing a cross-model check. The oracle consequence variant uses the with-patch reference label. Finally, priority-aware variants rank tasks according to $\hat{c}(x)\Delta(x)$ or $c(x)\Delta(x)$, where $\Delta(x)=p_{\mathrm{premium}}(x)-p_{\mathrm{cheap}}(x)$. Because $\Delta(x)$ is computed from observed outcomes, these variants are treated as analysis upper bounds rather than deployment-time strategies.

\textbf{Consequence routing protects the tasks that matter.}

overall accuracy remains nearly unchanged. This confirms that the
scheduler reallocates success toward costly failures rather than
uniformly improving the benchmark.

\textbf{Oracle consequence is close to the deployment-time predictor.}
The gap between the Qwen issue-only router and the with-patch oracle is small: $209.75$ versus $205.75$ cost-weighted loss. This matters because the with-patch oracle sees information unavailable at deployment time, while the issue-only predictor sees only the issue text and pre-solution file references. The downstream routing result therefore confirms that the predictor quality in \S\ref{sec:pred} is sufficient for allocation: it does not need perfect labels, only a useful ranking that avoids sending high-consequence tasks to the lowest tier. The priority-aware variants improve further, reaching a $30.7\%$ loss reduction with predicted consequence and a $33.0\%$ reduction with oracle consequence. They show the value of knowing where extra compute will change the answer, not a fully deployable solution. Appendix~\ref{app:marginal} shows that deployment-time predictors of $\Delta(x)$ are unreliable before execution, and Appendix~\ref{app:bootstrap} reports a split-half check showing that empirical priority rows are optimistic by roughly $6$--$7$ percentage points.

\textbf{Robustness, replication, and boundary conditions.}

Figure~\ref{fig:generalization} summarizes the main robustness and
boundary-condition evidence. The gain is strongest in SWE-style
software settings, where error costs are heterogeneous and difficulty
is not a reliable proxy for marginal compute gain. The main result is
stable under statistical, labeling, and cross-setting checks. Bootstrap
resampling, random-allocation variance, tiebreak analysis,
leave-one-class-2-out tests, and split-half estimates for the
priority-aware rows are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:bootstrap}. The
qualitative ordering is also stable under alternative monotone cost
mappings and continuous severity scores (Appendix~\ref{app:costmap}) and
under an alternative judge family with different severity calibration
(Appendix~\ref{app:judge2}).

Beyond these robustness checks, the full matched-budget allocation
comparison replicates on a disjoint database/ORM pool from SWE-bench
Verified using an independent 20-system tier: difficulty-aware routing
is again worse than random, consequence-aware routing improves over
random, and priority-aware routing is best
(Appendix~\ref{app:seconddomain}). The same ordering also holds when
model identity is fixed and the only allocated resource is the number of
best-of-$k$ attempts (Table~\ref{tab:withinmain} and
Appendix~\ref{app:within}). At the same time, the out-of-domain probes
show that consequence-aware routing is not universally superior: when
premium compute has little headroom, or when harder tasks are exactly
those that benefit most from additional reasoning, difficulty or
near-random routing can be competitive (Appendix~\ref{app:ood}).
Maintainer metadata and declared-stakes experiments further support the
interpretation that consequence is a deployment-cost variable rather
than a dataset-specific label artifact (Appendices~\ref{app:realcost}
and~\ref{app:context}).

These results do not imply that consequence should always dominate difficulty. Instead, the out-of-domain probes show that the cost-weighted objective predicts when the consequence advantage should disappear: it vanishes when costs are nearly homogeneous, no routing signal helps when the premium tier has no headroom, and difficulty routing wins when harder tasks are exactly where premium compute helps (Appendix~\ref{app:ood}). Thus, the gain should be read not as a universal advantage of consequence over difficulty, but as evidence that the right routing signal depends on the deployment cost structure and the marginal value of extra compute.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

A potential concern is that consequence-aware routing may simply reweight evaluation according to the same labels used for routing, but this applies primarily to oracle consequence results which serve only as upper bounds; the deployment-time scheduler instead relies on an issue-only predictor without access to gold patches, generated fixes, or execution outcomes, and our validations through human annotation, alternative labeling pipelines, maintainer-based severity analysis, and cost-model sensitivity confirm that consequence provides a distinct signal from difficulty (Appendices~\ref{app:iaa}--\ref{app:judge2}). Since routing occurs before solving, the method depends on estimating consequence from deployment-time inputs, and our predictor adopts a conservative operating point prioritizing avoidance of under-allocation on high-consequence tasks; while consequence can be estimated before execution, predicting the marginal benefit of additional computation remains substantially harder, motivating the use of consequence for ex-ante routing and execution feedback for in-flight adaptation (Appendices~\ref{app:predictor}, \ref{app:marginal}). The proposed scheduler is not universally superior to difficulty-aware allocation; its advantage depends on heterogeneous error costs and the availability of useful additional computation, and when costs are nearly uniform, premium compute provides little benefit, or difficulty is strongly aligned with compute gains, difficulty-aware allocation can be competitive (Appendix~\ref{app:ood}). Our experiments focus on software engineering tasks where consequence heterogeneity is naturally observable and matched-budget evaluation is possible; although we validate the framework on additional software pools and controlled within-model allocation (Appendices~\ref{app:seconddomain}, \ref{app:within}), broader domains remain open, and future work could incorporate richer deployment context, learned cost models, and adaptive policies combining consequence prediction with online execution feedback.

\section{Conclusion}

Adaptive test-time compute is usually treated as a problem of
difficulty: spend more computation on tasks that are harder, less
certain, or more likely to fail. This paper argues that deployment
requires a second axis. Errors differ in consequence. A system should
therefore decide not only where it is likely to be wrong, but where
being wrong is costly.
We formulated this as a cost-weighted compute-allocation problem and
introduced a deployable consequence-aware scheduler. The scheduler
predicts the consequence of an incorrect answer from deployment-time
inputs, then routes higher-consequence tasks to stronger compute tiers
or more attempts under the same total budget. The base reasoning model
is unchanged; consequence-awareness is added entirely at the scheduling
layer.
The main empirical result is a reallocation of success, not a uniform
accuracy gain. On SWE-bench Lite, the deployment-time scheduler raises
high-consequence success from $19.4\%$ under random routing and
$29.0\%$ under deployment-time predicted difficulty to $51.1\%$,
while overall accuracy remains essentially unchanged. This reduces
cost-weighted loss by $21.8\%$ relative to difficulty-aware routing
and retains most of the oracle-consequence gain. A controlled
within-model experiment, where only best-of-$k$ attempts are
reallocated, reproduces the same ordering.
The results also clarify when consequence-aware routing should and
should not help. Consequence is useful when error costs are
heterogeneous and difficulty is not a reliable proxy for marginal
compute benefit. It should not dominate when costs are uniform, when
premium compute has no headroom, or when harder tasks are exactly the
ones that benefit most from extra compute. The appendix experiments
confirm these boundary conditions across alternative labels, cost
mappings, judges, a second software pool, out-of-domain probes,
sequential policies, online routing, and context-dependent severity.
The broader lesson is that test-time compute allocation should not be
optimized only for average accuracy. In deployment, the value of extra
reasoning depends on what happens if the model is wrong. Consequence is
therefore not a replacement for difficulty or marginal-gain estimation;
it is the missing cost term that determines where successful reasoning
matters most.

\section{External Validity: Maintainer-Revealed Severity}
\label{app:realcost}

This appendix tests whether the consequence labels align with
maintainer behavior in the real repositories. The premise is that
maintainers reveal severity through costly actions such as backporting
fixes to stable release branches.

\paragraph{Backport mining.}
Each SWE-bench Lite instance encodes its originating pull request. For
$297/300$ tasks with resolvable merge commits, we mined the
corresponding repositories for whether the fix was backported. We also
recorded PR open-to-merge duration, discussion intensity, and security
markers.

\paragraph{Backporting tracks consequence.}
Backport rate increases monotonically with consequence:
\[
8.3\% \ (5/60) \quad \text{for class }0,
\]
\[
20.6\% \ (40/194) \quad \text{for class }1,
\]
\[
41.9\% \ (18/43) \quad \text{for class }2.
\]
The ordered trend is significant ($\rho=+0.232$, $p=0.0001$), and the
class-$2$ vs rest odds ratio is $3.34$ (Fisher $p=0.0009$). Part of
the pooled association may reflect repository composition, so we also
report stratified checks. A Cochran--Mantel--Haenszel test within
repositories gives pooled OR $1.76$ ($p=0.14$), and restricting to
repositories that backport at all preserves the monotone gradient
($19.2\%/36.3\%/47.4\%$) with an ordered trend
$\rho=+0.175$ ($p=0.024$). Thus, the pooled signal is not solely a
repository-mix artifact, although the repository-stratified contrast is
underpowered.

\paragraph{Null signals.}
PR open-to-merge duration does not separate consequence classes
(medians $2.0/1.9/1.6$ days; Kruskal--Wallis $p=0.56$). Comment
intensity also does not separate classes (medians $4/5/3$;
$p=0.19$). Security markers are too rare in SWE-bench Lite to be
informative. These nulls are useful: they suggest that backporting
captures severity more directly than general process friction.

\paragraph{Django Trac metadata.}
For the $114$ Django tasks, issue-tracker metadata provides an
LLM-independent anchor. Maintainer-assigned issue type tracks the
consequence label: the fraction labeled as genuine ``Bug'' rises from
$26.7\%$ at class~0 to $69.7\%$ at class~1 and $78.8\%$ at class~2
(ordered trend $\rho=+0.282$, $p=0.002$). The ``easy pickings''
difficulty flag is approximately orthogonal to consequence
($\rho=-0.12$, n.s.). Thus, maintainer behavior supports the paper's
central separation between consequence and difficulty.

\section{Why Difficulty-Aware Allocation Fails}
\label{app:whyfail}

This appendix provides the full marginal-gain analysis summarized in
\S\ref{sec:main}.

\paragraph{Marginal gain.}
Let
\[
\Delta\mathrm{success}(x)
=
p_{\mathrm{premium}}(x)-p_{\mathrm{cheap}}(x).
\]
This quantity measures where premium compute changes the outcome.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig_marginal.pdf}
\caption{\textbf{Difficulty and marginal gain on SWE-bench Lite.}
Marginal gain collapses on the hardest tasks, producing
$\rho(\mathrm{difficulty},\Delta\mathrm{success})=-0.78$
($p<10^{-60}$).}
\label{fig:marginal-app}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Difficulty anti-selects marginal gain.}
On SWE-bench Lite,
\[
\rho(\mathrm{difficulty},\Delta\mathrm{success})=-0.78,
\qquad p<10^{-60}.
\]
Many of the hardest tasks remain unsolved even at the premium tier, so
upgrading them consumes compute without changing outcomes.

\paragraph{Not only a never-solved artifact.}
There are $76/300$ tasks solved by no system. Removing them, the
correlation remains strongly negative:
\[
\rho=-0.489,\qquad p<10^{-14}.
\]
Thus, the pattern is not purely caused by the never-solved floor.

\paragraph{Deployment-time difficulty estimates.}
Issue-only solvability forecasts from two model families produce
difficulty estimates whose correlation with marginal gain is weak and
not significant: $-0.087$ and $+0.058$ on Lite, and $-0.051$ and
$-0.144$ on Verified. Routing by these deployment-time difficulty
estimates lands near random and below consequence routing.

\paragraph{Consequence and marginal gain.}
Consequence is nearly uncorrelated with marginal gain:
\[
\rho(\mathrm{consequence},\Delta\mathrm{success})=+0.09
\quad \text{(n.s.)}.
\]
Consequence therefore does not predict where compute helps. It predicts
where help is valuable if it occurs.

\paragraph{Consequence is not reducible to confidence.}
We also test whether consequence is merely a proxy for cross-model
disagreement. Let
\[
Y(x)=c(x)\Delta\mathrm{success}(x)
\]
be the cost-weighted marginal value of upgrading task $x$. We regress
$Y$ on difficulty, pass-rate variance, and consequence. Since $Y$
contains $c(x)$ by construction, this analysis should be read only as
a non-collinearity check, not as evidence that consequence predicts
marginal gain. The result shows that consequence is not absorbed by
difficulty or pass-rate variance.

worst and priority-aware best, while consequence-only matches random
because DeepSeek assigns class~2 to most of the pool, flattening cost
heterogeneity.

\paragraph{Why the consequence-only margin can collapse.}
This collapse is predicted by the objective. Consequence routing can
beat random only by concentrating premium compute on tasks that carry
disproportionate cost weight. When a judge marks most tasks as high
consequence, the top-quartile weight share approaches the uniform
baseline of $0.25$, leaving little room for a consequence ranking to
outperform random. In our judge-swap analysis, the top-quartile weight
share tracks the consequence-over-random margin: settings with strong
cost heterogeneity produce a larger consequence advantage, while the
DeepSeek Verified setting flattens the weights and therefore collapses
the consequence-only margin.

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
Judge swaps reveal a threshold-calibration issue, not a collapse of
the construct. When a judge flattens the cost distribution by marking
most tasks high consequence, consequence-only routing has little room
to outperform random. This is consistent with the objective: the value
of consequence routing depends on perceived cost heterogeneity.

\section{Context-Conditional Consequence}
\label{app:context}

Consequence depends on deployment context. The same error can be low
severity in a hobby project and high severity in a payment or
safety-critical system. This appendix treats context as an explicit
input:
\[
c(x) \rightarrow c(x,\mathrm{context}).
\]

\paragraph{Setup.}
For $100$ stratified SWE-bench Lite tasks, we elicit continuous
severity scores under four declared deployment contexts: a personal
hobby project, an internal business tool with human review, a
payment-processing service, and a safety-critical industrial control
system. We use two judge families, \texttt{deepseek-chat} and GPT-4o,
in both with-patch and issue-only modes, producing $1600$ labels.

\paragraph{Severity shifts monotonically with context.}
Mean severity rises monotonically along the stakes ladder. For
DeepSeek with-patch labels, the means are
$22.9 \to 26.2 \to 50.6 \to 64.7$. For GPT-4o, they are
$27.4 \to 28.4 \to 34.2 \to 42.5$. The shift is often monotone within
individual tasks as well: non-decreasing severity appears for
$71\%$ of tasks under DeepSeek and $90\%$ under GPT-4o in judge mode.

\paragraph{Routing adapts to context.}
Using each context's with-patch severity as the weight and the same
context's issue-only severity as the routing signal, consequence
routing beats random in all eight context-by-judge cells
($+6.1\%$ to $+10.9\%$) and difficulty routing in all eight
($+17.5\%$ to $+23.5\%$). The selected premium sets differ across
contexts: overlap between the top-$25\%$ sets under hobby and
safety-critical contexts is only $36\%$ under DeepSeek and $52\%$
under GPT-4o.

\paragraph{Interpretation.}
Context-dependence is not a threat to consequence-aware allocation; it
is an input to it. The scheduler can condition on deployment context
when the deployer provides it.


\section{Deployment-Time Consequence-Only Scheduler}
\label{app:scheduler}

This appendix provides the implementation details of the
deployment-time consequence-only scheduler introduced in
\S\ref{sec:method} and used in our experiments.
The scheduler is intentionally lightweight: it does not modify the
underlying reasoning model, require additional training, or use
execution feedback. Instead, it only determines how an existing
compute budget is distributed across tasks based on predicted
consequence.

Given a set of tasks $X$, a deployment-time consequence predictor
$\hat{c}(x)$ assigns each task a consequence score. Tasks are then
ranked according to this score, and higher-ranked tasks are upgraded
to stronger compute tiers whenever additional budget is available.
The scheduler starts from the cheapest tier and greedily allocates
remaining budget to the tasks with the highest predicted consequence.

The procedure is designed to match the deployment setting considered
in this paper. At routing time, the scheduler only has access to
information available before solving, including the issue description
and pre-solution metadata used by the consequence predictor. It does
not access gold patches, generated solutions, execution outcomes, or
any post-hoc information. Therefore, the algorithm represents a pure
ex-ante compute allocation strategy.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Deployment-time consequence-only compute allocation}
\label{alg:cons-route}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Tasks $X$; deployment-time consequence predictor $\hat{c}$;
tiers $T_1,\ldots,T_K$ with costs $c(T_k)$; total budget $B$.

\STATE Compute consequence scores:
$s(x)\leftarrow\hat{c}(x)$ for all $x\in X$

\STATE Sort tasks in descending order of $s(x)$

\STATE Initialize all tasks with the cheapest tier:
$T(x)\leftarrow T_1$

\STATE Initialize current cost:
$C\leftarrow\sum_{x\in X}c(T_1)$

\FOR{$k=K$ down to $2$}
    \FOR{$x$ in sorted task order}
        \IF{$T(x)=T_1$ and
        $C+c(T_k)-c(T_1)\leq B$}
            \STATE Upgrade task $x$ to tier $T_k$
            \STATE Update budget:
            $C\leftarrow C+c(T_k)-c(T_1)$
        \ENDIF
    \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR

\RETURN Allocation $T$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The greedy procedure in Algorithm~\ref{alg:cons-route}  is not intended as an optimal solver for all
possible budget allocation problems. Its purpose is to provide a
simple and deployable scheduler that isolates the contribution of
consequence as a routing signal. More sophisticated optimization
methods can be incorporated when additional information, such as
execution feedback or marginal compute gains, becomes available.
